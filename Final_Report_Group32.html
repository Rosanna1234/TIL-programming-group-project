Introduction¶

Traffic accidents on national highways can cause injuries, fatalities, economic losses and disruptions to transport. Identifying sections that deserve closer safety investigation is therefore important for road authorities. However, the sections with the most recorded crashes are not necessarily the most accident-prone relative to the amount of traffic using them. A busy section may have a high crash count simply because many vehicles travel through it, whereas a quieter section may have fewer crashes but a higher rate per kilometre driven. Previous research also shows that crash frequency tends to increase with traffic volume, though not always proportionally (Høye & Hesjevoll, 2020).

This project examines national-highway carriageway sections in South Holland during 2025. Building on earlier Dutch road-safety benchmarking (Schepers et al., 2020), it distinguishes the total burden of registered crashes from exposure-adjusted rates and considers whether road and traffic characteristics help explain differences across sections. The motivation is to provide a more informative basis for identifying potentially problematic locations, without assuming that observed crash patterns alone establish their causes.

South Holland is a relevant study area because its motorway network around Rotterdam and The Hague is among the busiest in the Netherlands and contains many closely spaced interchanges, on-ramps, off-ramps and connectors. This makes it a setting in which the difference between "many crashes" and "a high crash rate" is likely to matter for prioritisation.

The report is structured as follows. We first present the research objectives and questions, followed by the datasets and the data processing pipeline. Next, the methods are described, after which the results are presented per sub-question. The report ends with a discussion of the findings and limitations and a conclusion that answers the main research question.

Research Objectives¶

Objective. Identify national-highway carriageway sections in South Holland with relatively high crash frequencies in 2025 after taking traffic volume into account, and investigate traffic and carriageway characteristics associated with differences in crash frequency.

Main research question

Which highway carriageway sections in South Holland have the highest crash rates in 2025, given how busy they are, and which traffic and carriageway characteristics are associated with differences in crash frequency?

Sub-questions

  1. How does the ranking of carriageway sections by number of accidents differ from the ranking by risk (accidents per million vehicle-kilometres)?
  2. Which carriageway and traffic characteristics are associated with higher crash frequency after controlling for traffic volume and section length?
  3. Are the carriageway sections with the highest risk of injury and fatal accidents the same as those with the highest risk of all accidents?

Scope

  • Geographical scale: national-highway (A-road) carriageway sections located entirely within the province of South Holland.
  • Temporal scale: the calendar year 2025.
  • Unit of analysis: the INWEVA carriageway section (VBN_ID): one driving direction of a stretch of motorway, which can be a main carriageway, an on-ramp, an off-ramp or a connector between motorways.

Crash rates in this report are observed rates (registered crashes per million vehicle-kilometres), not direct probabilities of an individual driver's crash.

Key variables. Accident severity is taken from AP3_CODE: material damage only (UMS), injury (LET) and fatal (DOD). Traffic volume is the average 24-hour weekday intensity (AL_E_WK). The heavy-goods-vehicle (HGV) share is the sum of medium-heavy and heavy vehicles (L2_E_WK + L3_E_WK) divided by total traffic, and the night-time share is night traffic (AL_N_WK) divided by total traffic. INWEVA gives each traffic value a quality score (KWAL_AL). For most sections this is a number (up to 100), but 586 of the 1,208 sections used in the regression have the label correctie instead of a score.

Carriageway-type codes used in section names: HR = main carriageway (hoofdrijbaan), OPR = on-ramp (oprit), AFR = off-ramp (afrit). The codes starting with VB (VBR, VBD, VBS, VBK, VBI) are different kinds of connector roads between motorways (verbindingswegen); in SQ2 these are grouped together as "connector". Sections of the remaining type (PST) are not included in the regression.

Data processing pipeline¶

The raw files are processed by clean_data.py, which writes the cleaned files to cleaned_data/. The script performs the following steps:

  1. Select motorways. From the 8,763 INWEVA carriageway sections, the 6,920 sections on A-roads are kept.
  2. Select South Holland. Each carriageway section is linked to its NWB road sections, and these to municipalities. Only sections of which all NWB parts lie in a South Holland municipality are kept (1,228 sections). Sections crossing the provincial border (19) and sections without a usable municipality (17) are excluded.
  3. Clean traffic data. Weekday intensities per vehicle class and time of day are attached to the selected sections; 1,223 of the 1,228 sections have traffic data.
  4. Link accidents. Accidents are assigned to a section directly through their NWB road section (7,172 crashes). Accidents registered at a junction are assigned when the junction touches exactly one selected section (121 crashes). Junction accidents that touch several sections (10) cannot be assigned unambiguously and are excluded.
  5. Build the analysis dataset. Per section, crashes are counted by severity and combined with length, carriageway type and traffic data. Annual exposure, crash rates, HGV share and night-time share are calculated. The result is 05_analysis_ready_zh_motorways.csv, with one row per carriageway section (1,228 rows, 7,293 assigned crashes).

Methods¶

The analysis uses the cleaned BRON and INWEVA 2025 data described above, with national-highway carriageway sections in South Holland as the unit of analysis. The combined dataset contains 1,228 sections, with traffic data available for 1,223 sections. The mean section length is 766 m (median 503 m), while the mean number of registered crashes is 5.94 (median 1). Among sections with traffic data, average daily traffic is approximately 22,987 vehicles (median 15,300). In total, the dataset contains 7,293 assigned crashes, including 707 injury crashes and 11 fatal crashes.

To compare sections with different traffic volumes and lengths, annual traffic exposure is estimated in vehicle-kilometres:

$$ E_i = 365\,T_i\,\frac{L_i}{1000}, $$

where $T_i$ represents average daily traffic and $L_i$ is section length in metres. Overall crash rates and injury/fatal crash rates are calculated as:

$$ R_i = \frac{C_i}{E_i}\times 10^6, \qquad R_i^{IF} = \frac{I_i+F_i}{E_i}\times 10^6, $$

where $C_i$ is the total number of crashes, and $I_i$ and $F_i$ represent injury and fatal crashes, respectively. Both rates are expressed per million vehicle-kilometres. For Sub-question 2, additional explanatory variables include carriageway type, heavy-goods-vehicle (HGV) share, and night-time traffic share. HGV share is calculated from medium-heavy and heavy vehicle counts relative to total traffic, while night-time share represents the proportion of traffic occurring at night.

Each sub-question applies a different analytical approach:

Analysis Sample Method
SQ1: Crash counts vs. rates 1,228 sections for crash-count rankings; 849 sections for rate comparisons Top-10 rankings, ranking overlap, Spearman correlation, exposure scatterplot, and pooled carriageway-type rates
SQ2: Carriageway characteristics 1,208 sections with usable traffic and carriageway data Negative Binomial regression controlling for traffic volume and section length, incidence-rate ratios, and robustness checks
SQ3: Overall vs. injury and fatal rates 849 sections Top-10 rankings, ranking overlap, Spearman correlation, and rate comparison scatterplot

For SQ1 and SQ3, sections with fewer than 1 million annual vehicle-kilometres are excluded from rate comparisons to reduce extreme values caused by very low traffic exposure. This threshold retains 849 sections and 7,103 crashes (97.4% of all assigned crashes). Alternative thresholds of 0.5, 2, and 5 million vehicle-kilometres were also examined. SQ2 does not apply this threshold in its primary regression because traffic volume and section length are included as control variables.

Two methodological considerations should be noted. First, annual exposure is estimated by multiplying the supplied weekday traffic intensity by 365, assuming it reasonably represents daily traffic throughout the year. Second, exposure-adjusted rates describe observed accident frequency, while regression estimates identify statistical associations after controlling for selected characteristics. Neither measure independently establishes causality.

SQ2: Carriageway and Traffic Characteristics¶

A Negative Binomial regression was fitted to total crash counts, with log traffic intensity and log section length as controls, alongside carriageway type, heavy-goods-vehicle (HGV) share and night-traffic share. The sample includes 1,208 sections and 7,282 crashes. Crash-count variance (174.9) greatly exceeds the mean (6.0); the Negative Binomial fit is substantially better than Poisson (AIC 4,979.1 versus 7,255.6). Results for characteristics are expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRRs): an IRR below 1 indicates fewer expected crashes conditional on the included controls.

Traffic and length are positively but less than proportionally related to crash counts (estimated log coefficients 0.82 and 0.69, respectively). Connectors have about 67% fewer expected crashes than comparable main carriageways (IRR 0.33), while on- and off-ramp differences are not statistically significant. HGV share is not significant; night-traffic share has a small positive association (IRR 1.026, $p=0.043$), whose magnitude varies across robustness samples. Adding road and traffic characteristics improves model fit beyond traffic and length alone (likelihood-ratio statistic 160.7, $p<0.001$).

Discussion¶

The results show that crash counts and exposure-adjusted crash rates measure different aspects of road safety. SQ1 finds no overlap between the ten highest-count and the ten highest-rate sections. Busy main carriageways produce many crashes, while short ramps often show higher rates relative to estimated vehicle-kilometres. Neither measure should be interpreted as a complete ranking of underlying danger: high counts indicate where the largest total crash reduction could be achieved, whereas high rates signal locations that may merit closer inspection.

The regression in SQ2 helps to interpret this difference. The estimated coefficients for log traffic (0.82) and log length (0.69) are both below 1, meaning that crashes increase less than proportionally with traffic and section length. This is in line with the meta-analysis of Høye and Hesjevoll (2020). A simple rate per vehicle-kilometre assumes a proportional relationship, so it will tend to be higher for short and less busy sections. This explains why ramps rank high in SQ1 while the regression finds no statistically significant independent on-/off-ramp effect once traffic volume and length are controlled for. Connectors have significantly lower expected crash counts than comparable main carriageways, the HGV share is not significant, and the night-share association is small and sensitive to the sample used.

SQ3 adds a severity perspective. Most high injury/fatal-rate sections also have high overall rates, but several individual sections move sharply in the ranking, such as the A4 connector at Zoeterwoude (111th to 5th). Looking only at total counts or overall rates could therefore overlook locations with disproportionately severe outcomes, although rates based on one or two serious crashes are uncertain.

Limitations. The conclusions are constrained by several factors:

  • Only one year of registered accidents is used, and not all accidents are registered in BRON, so rankings of individual sections are sensitive to random variation.
  • Annual exposure multiplies the average weekday intensity by 365, which overestimates traffic on weekends and may slightly underestimate rates.
  • For 586 of the 1,208 sections in the regression, the traffic data have the INWEVA label correctie instead of a numeric quality score. Because it is not clear how reliable these values are, we repeated the regression without them; the main connector result still holds.
  • Sections crossing the provincial border (19) and junction crashes that could not be assigned to a single section (10) were excluded.
  • Results are observational associations, not causal effects; local factors such as geometry, weaving length, speed limits and safety measures were not included.

Future work could combine multiple years of data, use a correction factor for weekend traffic, quantify uncertainty in hotspot rankings (for example with confidence intervals or Empirical Bayes estimates), and add local road-design information before selecting interventions.

Conclusion¶

This project asked which national-highway carriageway sections in South Holland have the highest crash rates in 2025, given how busy they are, and which traffic and carriageway characteristics are associated with differences in crash frequency.

Highest crash rates. After accounting for traffic exposure, the highest observed crash rates occur on short ramps around Rotterdam and Vlaardingen, such as A20 a (AFR) Rotterdam-Noord (8.90), A4 b (OPR) Vlaardingen-Oost (8.34) and A20 d (OPR) Rotterdam-Schiebroek (5.95 crashes per million vehicle-kilometres). None of these ten highest-rate sections is among the ten sections with the most crashes, which are long and busy main carriageways on the A4, A15, A16 and A20. Busy sections with large crash totals and sections with disproportionately high rates are therefore not the same places (SQ1).

Associated characteristics. After controlling for traffic volume and section length, connectors have about 67% fewer expected crashes than main carriageways. A higher share of night-time traffic is associated with slightly more crashes (+2.6% per percentage point), but this effect is small and varies between samples. On-ramps, off-ramps and the HGV share show no statistically significant difference. Crash counts increase less than proportionally with traffic and length, which partly explains why short ramps reach high rates (SQ2).

Severity. Seven of the ten sections with the highest injury/fatal crash rates are also among the ten highest overall-rate sections, but some sections, such as the A4 connector at Zoeterwoude, only stand out when severity is considered (SQ3).

Overall, the findings suggest that Rijkswaterstaat and other road authorities should consider crash totals, exposure-adjusted rates and injury/fatal rates together when deciding which sections merit further investigation. The results highlight potential priorities rather than proving that particular road features cause crashes. Using several years of data and additional local safety information would strengthen decisions about targeted improvements.
